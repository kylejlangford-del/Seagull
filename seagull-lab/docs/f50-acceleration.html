How it was taken

Every one-second sample in every race in the bundle, with the acceleration read as a centred difference over the samples either side. A boat in a turn is not accelerating in any sense a cone can use, so anything with a rate of turn over 3°/s is thrown away; that filter and the missing-channel checks together drop 64,650 of 174,123 samples. What is left is binned three ways: by the speed the boat is already doing, by wind strength, and by true wind angle.

The number wanted is not the average. Most of the time a boat is not trying to accelerate at all, so the average is a statement about how often the fleet is coasting, not about what the boat can do. Each cell is therefore read at the top of its distribution.

What it says

  • Wind is the biggest term — about four times across the range. At 40–50 km/h boat speed and 60–90° off the wind, the same boat gains 0.67 km/h per second in under 20 km/h of breeze and 2.74 in over 36. A cone drawn off one acceleration figure is wrong by a factor of four between a light day and a windy one.
  • Angle matters most when you are slow. At 10–20 km/h in 20–28 km/h of breeze, 30–60° off the wind gives 0.65 and 120–180° gives 0.25 — two and a half times. Deep and slow there is no apparent wind to build on, which is exactly the state a boat is in when it has run out of room and turned down the line.
  • There is a soft patch at 50–60 km/h. Taken across all winds and angles the 90th percentile runs 1.78 at 40–50, dips to 1.46 at 50–60, and climbs again to 1.75 and 2.21 through 60–80. That is the transition region, and it is the part of the pull a linear ramp gets wrong. Inside a single wind band the pattern is noisier — the dip is clear, the recovery above 60 is not in every column.
  • The ceiling is not in this table. Even the 90th percentile never goes negative, because a percentile that high never does. The cone caps the integration at the polar target for the angle and wind it is sailing, which the tool already has.

How the cone uses it

For each sampled heading the cone integrates forward from the speed you are doing now, in one-second steps, to the gun:

v ← min(v + a(v, tws, twa) · dt, polar target)

and the distance run is the sum. The locus of those endpoints is the cone — where you could be at T=0 if you pulled the trigger now and held that heading. Where it crosses the start line is the stretch of line you can make on time; the part of it beyond the line is where you would be early.

Two things it deliberately does not model: the time to turn from your current heading onto the sampled one, and dirty air. Both make the cone smaller, never bigger, so the cone as drawn is the optimistic edge — which is the right way for it to be wrong, because a cone that promises less than the boat can do gets ignored.
